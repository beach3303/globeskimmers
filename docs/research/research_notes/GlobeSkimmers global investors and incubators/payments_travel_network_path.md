## Key Question 1 — Corporate programs that take early fintech/travel startups (Visa, Mastercard, Apple/Google Tap to Pay, Stripe/Adyen/Square, Alipay+, GCash/Maya, UPI/PromptPay/ASEAN QR, Wise/Nium/Thunes, Amex/Capital One/Chase)

### Takeaway
The card networks' flagship startup programs (Visa Africa Fintech Accelerator, Visa Fintech Fast Track, Mastercard Start Path) are gated at seed-to-Series-A with a live, revenue-generating product or at least $3M raised; the US Visa Everywhere Initiative round appears dormant (its domain no longer resolves). By contrast, the "tap to pay on phone" door is already open to any small app today: Apple's Tap to Pay on iPhone requires only a PSP integration (Stripe, Adyen, Square and dozens of others) plus an Apple entitlement, and Stripe Connect lets a platform enable its own sub-merchants — but it is live in only 7 Asia-Pacific markets (AU, HK, JP, MY, NZ, SG, TW) and not in the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Korea or India. In those markets the acceptance rails are QR (Alipay+, QR Ph, QRIS, PromptPay, Nexus), which are reached as an acquirer/merchant partner, not as a wallet.

### Inferences
- For a pre-seed, pre-revenue app the card-network programs are 1–2 funding rounds away: Start Path wants a live, revenue-generating product and prior institutional funding; Visa Fintech Fast Track wants ≥$3M raised and is US-only for Visa Direct. The realistic first touchpoints are the regional VEI competitions where they still run (LAC, Africa) and Visa's developer portal — not the accelerators.
- Enabling merchants with Tap to Pay on iPhone/Android does not require any license of your own: the PSP (Stripe, Adyen, Square…) carries certification and the money; the app needs the Apple entitlement and, as a Connect platform, must sign Apple's Acceptance Platform terms. Cost of entry is Stripe's 2.7% + 5¢ + $0.10 per tap, with a 0.25% platform take if you set your own merchant pricing.
- The flagship Southeast Asian tourist destinations where cash-only small merchants are most common (Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia) are precisely where Apple Tap to Pay is **not** available; in those markets the merchant-side path is QR acceptance as an acquiring/merchant partner of Alipay+ (which markets a "one integration for multiple payment apps" product to acquirers) or via local PSP/aggregators that expose GCash/Maya/QR Ph.
- A foreign consumer app cannot "join" UPI, QRIS, PromptPay or Nexus as a wallet; those schemes are bank/central-bank membership networks. The only documented foreign-tourist on-ramp is the UPI One World PPI wallet, issued by licensed Indian PPI issuers, and so far only in event pilots.

## Key Question 2 — Fintech and financial-inclusion investors who back early; development-finance and philanthropic money for merchant digitization (who funds startups vs. only NGOs)

### Takeaway
Fintech-specialist VCs that genuinely write pre-seed/seed checks include Better Tomorrow Ventures ($140M Fund III, Oct 2025), QED (states "pre-seed to IPO" but early-stage initial checks are $3–10M), Nyca (seed to Series B), Flourish (evergreen, "early-stage", emerging markets + US), and the Accion/Quona family (Quona mostly leads Series A; Accion Venture Lab is the seed arm). The philanthropic and development-finance pools — Gates Foundation, Better Than Cash Alliance, Mastercard Strive, Visa Foundation — fund governments, NGOs, open-source infrastructure and intermediary funds, not consumer startups directly; IFC is the exception that invests directly in tech companies but at later stages and mostly in emerging markets.

### Cited Findings

## Key Question 3 — Regulatory reality for a US consumer app and the credible 5-year ladder (MTL costs/timelines, sponsor-bank/PSP model, EMI abroad, who does each step for small startups and what it costs)

### Takeaway
Nationwide US money-transmitter licensing is a multi-year, seven-figure undertaking (49 states, 21–54 weeks per state, bonds $10k–$1M+, net worth up to $1M per state) — which is why every consumer travel-payments product of note (Wise, Revolut, Nubank's US launch, Grab, Gojek) started on a partner's license or acquired an e-money license abroad before any bank charter. For a small startup the entire first four rungs — rewards, a branded card (Stripe Issuing/Lithic/Highnote with a sponsor bank; Visa Fast Track at ≥$3M raised), merchant tap-to-pay (Stripe Connect/Adyen/Square), and cross-border payouts/wallet acceptance (Nium, Thunes, Alipay+ as an acquiring partner) — are available by contract with no license of your own; the fifth rung (joining a national QR or Nexus scheme as a participant) is a bank/EMI-level step in each country.

### Cited Findings

**US money transmitter licensing (MTL) — scope, cost, time**
- "Forty-nine US states (sans Montana) regulate (i.e., require licensure for) money transmitters"; it is "a felony to engage in money transmission without a license" where required; "Most of the states require a money transmitter surety bond" ranging from about $25,000 to over $1 million; FinCEN requires MSB registration; internet/mobile payment services must obtain a state license to serve residents — [Wikipedia: Money transmitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_transmitter)
- State data index ("Last updated November 26, 2026"): government fees "$300 to $5,000" per state; processing "21 to 54 weeks" (most states 21–42; California and New York 25–54); surety bonds $10,000 (Idaho, Washington, Wyoming) to $1,000,000 (Pennsylvania); net-worth floors $10,000+ (Washington) to $1,000,000 (Utah); 12-month renewals; Montana n/a — [Cornerstone Licensing](https://cornerstonelicensing.com/licensing-data/money-transmitter)
- A 2026 guide estimates $50,000 to $300,000 per state all-in, 3 to 18 months per state, and "$1M–$5M+" for a phased 10–15-state rollout — [Buckingham Capital Consulting](https://www.buckinghamcapitalconsulting.com/post/how-to-obtain-a-us-money-transmitter-licence-2026-state-by-state-guide); FinCEN MSB registration "costs nothing and must be filed within 180 days of starting operations" — [Digipay.guru](https://www.digipay.guru/blog/money-transmitter-license-usa-compliance-guide/); California's bond can reach $250,000 to $7,000,000 — [Fintechspecs](https://fintechspecs.com/blog/money-transmitter-license-cost-by-state/); a consultant's all-in 50-state estimate is $572,530 (sales estimate including prep fees) — [Faisal Khan](https://faisalkhan.com/pricing/us-state-money-transmitter-license-pricing). Figures conflict across guides (e.g., New York application fee $3,000 vs $5,000).

**The sponsor-bank / program-manager model after the 2023–24 BaaS shakeout**
- Sponsor banks now ask for a signed matrix assigning compliance duties among the fintech, any BaaS middleware and the bank; "The 2023-2024 wave of consent orders against BaaS sponsor banks reshaped how sponsors evaluate FinTech partners"; expectations include a documented BSA program with named officers and quarterly metrics reviews to the bank — [AZDV sponsor-bank readiness checklist (May 2026)](https://azdv.co/blog/2026/05/baas-sponsor-bank-readiness-checklist/)
- The fintech typically handles onboarding and first-line monitoring under the bank's policies while the bank keeps oversight and filing; agreements cover change approval, BSA/AML and OFAC, Reg E error resolution, incident notification, audit and regulator access — [Canarie](https://www.canarie.ai/blog/sponsor-bank-agreement-obligations-before-signing)
- Banks evaluate concentration risk and exit/wind-down plans; "the BaaS shakeout has not produced a single universal checklist"; a sponsor under a fresh consent order can have new-program approvals frozen — [Banking Industry Review](https://bankingindustryreview.com/sponsor-bank-oversight-after-the-baas-shakeout/)

**Card issuing for small programs — who, and what it costs**
- Stripe Issuing publishes "no setup fee, no stated monthly minimum, and $0.10 per virtual card and $3.50 per physical card in the US"; "Stripe's partner bank is not selectable by the program operator." Lithic does not publish pricing — deals "combine interchange-share splits with per-card issuance fees and monthly API volume minimums", with a fully managed path (sponsor bank included) or processor-only (bring your own BIN sponsor). Highnote runs its own BIN sponsorship. Sponsor-bank economics generally: "a few basis points of volume plus monthly minimums of roughly $2,000 to $25,000 at small scale"; a program "below 10 million dollars in annual card spend is usually cheapest on Stripe Issuing or a managed Lithic setup" — [Fintechspecs: Marqeta vs Lithic vs Stripe Issuing](https://fintechspecs.com/blog/marqeta-vs-lithic-vs-stripe-issuing/); [Fintechspecs: Marqeta alternatives](https://fintechspecs.com/blog/marqeta-alternatives-card-issuing-platforms/); [The Platform PM: costs](https://www.theplatformpm.com/field-guides/card-issuing-program/money)
- Visa Fintech Fast Track requires "at least $3M in funding" and routes fintechs to "BIN sponsors, processors, and/or program managers" — [Visa Partner](https://partner.visa.com/site/programs/fintech-program.html); "Fintechs generally issue on Visa through a BIN sponsor, since becoming a direct member of the card scheme requires a banking licence and substantial capital" — [Decta](https://www.decta.com/company/media/mastercard-vs-visa-for-new-issuers)
- Nium offers card programs "in 30+ markets with no BIN sponsor" — [Nium](https://www.nium.com/)

**Merchant acceptance for small startups (Tap to Pay via a PSP)**
- Apple requires a PSP first and an Apple entitlement; the PSP "is responsible for all the necessary certifications" — [Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/tap-to-pay/); Stripe: 2.7% + 5¢ in-person, +$0.10 per Tap to Pay authorization, +1.5% international cards, 0.25% platform fee if the platform sets its own pricing — [Stripe Pricing](https://stripe.com/pricing); Connect platforms enabling sub-merchants must accept Apple's Acceptance Platform terms — [Stripe Legal](https://stripe.com/legal/apple-acceptance-platform)

**E-money licenses abroad**
- EU EMI: "€350,000 in initial capital, fully paid-up at authorisation"; ongoing own funds "at least 2% of the average outstanding electronic money" with a €350,000 floor — [Crassula EMI guide](https://crassula.io/guides/licenses/electronic-money-institution/); Lithuania has a restricted-licence track with no fixed capital minimum but capped at EUR 900k per month outstanding e-money and domestic-only passporting, with a 2-month decision period; the standard track is a "6 to 12 month timeline" — [Crassula Lithuania EMI](https://crassula.io/guides/licenses/lithuania-emi/); Bulgaria's central bank still grants EMI licences in 2026 — [Innovires](https://www.innovires.com/en/blog/emi-licence-bulgaria-psd3.html). No full cost breakdown was found; sources are licensing consultancies.
- Philippines: BSP extended its moratorium on new licences for non-bank electronic money issuers to end-2024 (article Dec 14, 2023) — [Fintech News PH search results](https://fintechnews.ph/?s=EMI+moratorium); BSP set higher liquidity and capital requirements for EMIs with significant operations (Feb 9, 2023); Revolut reportedly plans to apply for Philippine "digital bank and electronic money issuer (EMI) licences" (Oct 6, 2026); BSP is "proposing new rules to open up digital payments to more third-party providers" (Nov 24, 2026) — [Fintech News PH search results](https://fintechnews.ph/?s=electronic+money+issuer); BSP lifted its moratorium on new digital banking licences (Jan 6, 2025) — [Fintech News PH](https://fintechnews.ph/?s=EMI+moratorium)

**Why the big consumer travel-payments names partnered or licensed abroad first**
- Wise "is not a bank and does not lend out customer money"; US customer funds are protected through partner banks with FDIC insurance via Wise US Inc. — [Wikipedia: Wise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wise_(company))
- Revolut "received its EU banking licence as well as an EMI licence in December 2018", "facilitated by the Bank of Lithuania"; UK banking licence granted July 2024 with "mobilisation" restrictions; full UK licence 11 March 2026 — [Wikipedia: Revolut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolut)
- Nubank is "regulated and supervised by the Central Bank of Brazil"; Nu México converted from a SOFIPO to a bank (authorized July 2026, operating 6 Aug 2026); in the US it received OCC conditional approval in January 2026 and "launched U.S. operations on September 10, 2026, through a partnership with Lead Bank" — [Wikipedia: Nubank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubank)
- Grab launched GrabPay in November 2017, acquired payments startup Kudo (April 2017), invested in OVO (Nov 2018), formed Grab Financial in 2018, was granted a Singapore digital bank licence with Singtel in December 2020 and launched GXS Bank in August 2022 — [Wikipedia: Grab Holdings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grab_Holdings)
- Gojek's Go-Pay "gained a central bank license to use QR code payment" in May 2017, bought Kartuku, Midtrans and Mapan in December 2017, and took a majority stake in Coins.ph in January 2019 — [Wikipedia: Gojek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gojek)

### Inferences (the credible 5-year ladder, with who does each rung)
1. **Stamp partners / rewards & passes (no license):** closed-loop rewards and partner offers at stamped places need no MTL as long as no stored value is redeemable for cash; this is the stage corporate programs will engage at (Visa developer portal, regional VEI, Alipay+ "merchant" category). Source basis: Apple/Stripe/Visa pages above describe no licensing for these roles.
2. **Branded travel card via BaaS (no license of your own):** Stripe Issuing (published per-card pricing, no minimum) or a managed Lithic/Highnote program with its sponsor bank; expect sponsor-bank minimums of ~$2k–$25k/month and a documented BSA program; Visa Fast Track becomes available once ≥$3M is raised.
3. **Merchant acceptance in tourist towns via Tap to Pay (no license):** as a Stripe Connect / Adyen for Platforms / Square developer, onboard small merchants as connected accounts; works today in the US, Europe, Australia, Japan, Singapore, Malaysia, Hong Kong, Taiwan, NZ, Mexico, Brazil and most of LatAm — but not in the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Korea, India.
4. **QR acceptance in Southeast Asia (as an acquirer/merchant partner, under a local PSP's license):** Alipay+ "acquiring service providers" and "merchants" tracks, Thunes "Accept Mobile Wallets", or local aggregators exposing GCash/Maya/QR Ph; still no license of your own, but local entity and KYB are required by GCash/Maya.
5. **Own licenses (5+ years, only if volumes justify):** EU EMI (€350k capital, 6–12 months) or a Lithuanian restricted EMI as the cheapest regulated foothold; US MTL footprint ($1–5M+, 12–18+ months); Philippine EMI only if BSP's moratorium posture changes (last documented extension to end-2024; current status unverified). Joining Nexus/QRIS/PromptPay/QR Ph as a participant is a bank/EMI-level step via central banks.
- The companies the founder would be compared to all followed this order: product → partner rails → foreign e-money licence → bank charter 5–9 years in (Revolut 2015→2018 EMI/bank→2026 full UK; Grab 2012→2017 GrabPay→2020 licence; Nubank 2013→2026 US via Lead Bank).

## Key Question 5 — The "biggest airline / biggest travel network" ambition: capital-light networks (Trip.com, Booking, Expedia, Airbnb) vs. asset-heavy airlines (JetBlue, Breeze), and what investors say is fundable from a consumer-app start

### Takeaway
The world's largest travel networks were built capital-light — Priceline bought Booking.com for $133M and Active Hotels for $161M, Ctrip IPO'd on $75M and later bought Skyscanner for £1.4B, Airbnb went from a $20k YC check to a $7.2M Series A — and none of them own planes or hotels; airlines are the opposite: JetBlue needed $128M before its first flight, Breeze needed >$300M of equity before 2022 plus hundreds of millions in secured aircraft debt in 2025–26, and both were founded by a serial airline operator. In the 2026 travel-funding market (Q1 2026 deal count at a decade low; investors "extremely selective"; capital concentrating in B2B/infrastructure and AI), the fundable arc from a consumer app is the network/marketplace path with embedded fintech, not aircraft ownership.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences (frank assessment)
- "Biggest travel network" is a fundable long-game framing for a consumer app because the comparables (Booking, Trip.com, Airbnb, Klook, Traveloka, GetYourGuide) were all built on commissions and acquisitions with no owned supply; investors will accept it as a 10–15-year narrative if the near-term plan is demand aggregation with proven unit economics.
- "Biggest airline" is not fundable from a consumer-app starting point: the only recent US airline startup needed a five-airline founder, >$300M equity before launch, and hundreds of millions of aircraft debt afterward; no evidence was found of any software or consumer-app founder raising for an airline. The honest pitch is "biggest travel network/supplier by distribution", and if the founder means airline-like supply, "partner airline" or "virtual carrier/brand" with a licensed operator (not researched; see gaps).
- Payments as the monetization layer is consistent with how the comparables and the fintech VCs think (embedded finance per QED; travel/airline verticals per Nium; Hopper's fintech features). The credible wording is "the trusted payments and acceptance layer for travelers", built on partner rails first, with licenses later only where volume justifies — not "own the biggest payment system", which no investor source in this research describes as a startup outcome.
- The 2026 market data argue for showing B2B/infrastructure optionality (merchant tools, acceptance, data) alongside the consumer passport, since pure consumer growth stories are explicitly out of favor with travel investors this year.
