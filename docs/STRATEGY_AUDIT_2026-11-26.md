# Globeskimmers, the honest audit: how big, which path, what to buy

Written 2026-11-26 for the founder, after she asked: can this become a hundreds-of-billions company, in which direction, what could we sell, what could we buy, and are there better options than my dreams. Money figures are 2026 snapshots or the latest public round; private-company figures marked ~ are approximate and should be verified before they go in a deck.

## The one-paragraph answer

Yes, there is a path to a company worth hundreds of billions, and it does not run through an airline. It runs through the thing Globeskimmers already does that nobody else does: proving a person was really there. Proof of presence is the seed of a loyalty currency (stamps that pay), a loyalty currency is the seed of a payments network (a card, then a wallet, then acceptance at small places), and a payments network wrapped in travel and trust is the most valuable kind of company in this industry. The one company that already contains every dream on your list except the planes is American Express: freight courier in 1850, traveler's cheques in 1891, charge card in 1958, lounges, a travel agency, a brand people pay to carry. It is worth about $230 to 250 billion today. The sentence to build toward is "the American Express of the passport generation." Planes are what a company like that buys in year fifteen, not the way it gets there.

The lesson in the table: the top of the market is networks that take a slice of every transaction or own the currency people spend. The bottom is hardware. American Airlines' loyalty program has been valued at several times the airline that runs it. Airlines are credit cards with wings, and the wings are the expensive part.

## What you actually own today

Assets, stated plainly: a verification engine (a stamp only inside a real footprint, or from a photo with its own GPS and date), a catalog of 26,415 stampable places in 100+ countries that you own and no API can take away, 1,500 illustrated stamps and a visual language people recognise, a passport object people want to fill, apps live on both stores and now in the browser, airport and land-border sensing, City Cards and postcards, and a founder who shipped all of it alone in eight months. The honest gap: 96 accounts, 29 active in the last 30 days, no revenue. Everything below assumes the next twelve months turn that into a retention curve.

Three notes on the table.

Path D is the one you ruled out, and it is also the strongest consumer wedge you have. Tripadvisor is worth about $2B because nobody believes its reviews. A review that can only be written by a person whose stamp proves they stood there is the first new trust primitive in travel since the star rating. You do not have to sell bookings to use it; you can license the trust. But if you ever want the Booking-sized outcome on the consumer side, this is the door, and it should be a conscious re-decision at scale, not an accident.

Path E is how you get to "I sell a product I am proud of." The printed passport book of your own stamps is the obvious first product: high margin, zero inventory risk if printed on demand, and every copy is an advertisement. Luggage and apparel come after a hundred thousand members, not before. Away reached ~$1.4B in five years and then shrank; a brand without a network under it is fragile. A brand on top of a loyalty network is Amex.

Path F deserves a direct answer because it is the dream you named. Delta is the most valuable airline on earth and trades for less than one year of its revenue. The loyalty program inside it is worth more than half the company. The way to own "an airline" early is to own the currency airlines want: at ten million members who travel, every carrier will want your stamps in its program, and you will be sitting on the half of the airline that is worth something. Elon Musk's sequence was software first, Zip2 and PayPal, then the cash bought the rockets and the cars. Your sequence is proof, then points, then payments, then planes.

## What to buy, and when

Early, under $5M: travel-journal and stamp apps whose members would fill passports (the Polarsteps and Been class), domain names, small stamp-art studios. Middle, $20–200M: loyalty and rewards startups in travel, a regional travel wallet in Southeast Asia, a review company whose trust you can restore with verification. Late, billions: Tripadvisor (~$2B) or Yelp (~$2B) for the place graph and the audience, a minority stake in an airline loyalty program (the thing worth owning), a small carrier the size of Hawaiian ($1.9B including debt) if the trophy still matters at that point. Do not buy an airline before you own a currency. Do not buy a brand before you own a network.

1. "The American Express of the passport generation" is a bigger and more fundable sentence than "an airline." It contains payments, travel, trust, loyalty, products and brand, and it has a $230B precedent.
2. "Reviews only from people who were there" is a bigger consumer wedge than any product line. It is yours alone, because only you verify presence.
3. The Philippines is the first country where Globeskimmers can be the travel wallet, not just the passport: a diaspora of about ten million, a home population of 118 million, GCash and Maya as partners, and a government grant fund that opened this week. Win one country completely before winning the world a little.
4. LA28 is the stage, not the business. Official stamps at every venue (named factually, no rings, torch, emblem or mascot) put the passport in front of the world's travelers in one summer.
5. The founder story is a strength only if the company survives contact with the full-time rule. Every door in today's research asked it. The answer is a dated plan: full time on the first committed capital.

## How to become more powerful than the payment networks (founder's question, same day)

Not by building a better card network; nobody has beaten Visa at its own game in fifty years. The companies that rose above the networks owned something the networks do not and made them plumbing underneath. Visa and Mastercard own the rules and the default, not the money, the banks or the merchants; they take a toll on every transaction without carrying risk, and they do not know the person, the place or the reason. Apple Pay owns the tap and takes a fee from issuers without owning a network. Alipay and WeChat Pay skipped cards with QR and signed 150 million small merchants the networks never reached; Ant was headed above $300B before the state stopped it, which is the lesson in both directions. UPI and PIX are government rails built to shrink the networks' power; whoever owns the interface on top wins the user. Amex owns both sides in a closed loop and earns more per transaction than Visa.

What Globeskimmers owns that none of them have: proof of presence (a verified "this human is at this place now" signal, worth more than a card number because fraud is the networks' largest cost and intent their blindest spot), the traveler before the spend, the small places the networks never signed, and a currency both sides want. Nobody owns the tap across borders; that seat is empty.

The route, in order: (1) own the currency, stamps that pay, funded by places; (2) own the tap, one Globeskimmers tap or QR that works at a stamped place in any country, routed underneath to the cheapest rail there (Visa in Paris, Alipay+ in Bangkok, UPI in Delhi, PIX in Rio); (3) route around, pushing volume to cheap account-to-account and QR rails and handing the savings to small merchants, which with passport foot traffic is how cash-only places are won; (4) close the loop in travel economies the way Amex did. Then the networks need Globeskimmers more than it needs them. Caveats: about a hundred million traveling members and roughly a decade (Alipay fifteen years, Apple Pay ten), and power at that scale draws regulators, so partner with central-bank rails rather than fight them.

## Shorter than Alipay, and who pays (founder's question, same day)

Members never pay for a stamp. Stamps, the passport, postcards and cards stay free; the person is the asset, as the cardholder is for Amex and the renter for Bilt. The other side pays: places that want an official stamp, destinations, airports, airlines, brands sponsoring a country series, and later the bank that issues the card. A paid decorative cover is the only thing a member ever buys.

Why it can be shorter: Alipay and Apple Pay had to create the behavior and the hardware; today Tap to Pay runs on any phone through Stripe or Adyen, QR covers Asia, Alipay+ connects 150M merchants, so the acceptance layer is assembled in one to two years. Distribution is the only hard part. What compresses time: ride existing audiences (the Filipino diaspora and home country, a China series, the US list, each a country launch with creators and press); ride moments (LA28, Wrapped every December); build loops into the product (a postcard to a non-member, a shared page, a QR card are all invitations); let partners recruit (an official-stamp destination promotes it, an airport prints "stamp your arrival"); capital.

The arithmetic: Duolingo took about ten years to 100M monthly users, Polarsteps five years to 1M, Pokémon GO 100M in weeks on a borrowed brand. Ladder if retention holds: 1,000 by March 2027, 50,000 in 2027, 1M in the LA28 year, 10–25M by 2031, 100M around 2032–33: seven years instead of fifteen, and only if people who stamp once come back and bring someone. Retention is the number every rung is priced off. Claude builds the loops, partner tooling, reports, launches, measurement and raises; only the product can make a stranger open the app twice.
